Privacy Policy

Hush for iPhone and iPad · Effective 21 October 2026

Hush is an ad and tracker blocker made by Soumya Ranjan Mahunt ("I", "me"). This policy explains what data the app uses, where it goes, and the choices you have. Hush can block in three ways: Safari blocking, VPN mode and Hush Everywhere. Each handles your browsing differently, so each is described below.

In short: no account or sign-up, no analytics, no ads and no tracking. I don't collect any personal data, and I can't see which sites or apps you use. Safari blocking and VPN mode decide everything on your device. VPN mode sends lookups it doesn't block to Cloudflare's public DNS resolver, or to your own VPN provider if you add one. Hush Everywhere asks my filtering service about some addresses, but in an encrypted form the service can't read, so it never learns which address was asked about.

Data stored on your device

Hush keeps the following on your device only, in storage shared between the app and its extensions:

  • the hosts you add to your own blocklist;
  • your settings, such as which Safari blockers are paused, whether to block when unsure, and how often the blocklist is refreshed;
  • for VPN mode, the WireGuard configuration you import from your VPN provider (including its keys), saved in your device's VPN settings, and running counts of lookups checked and blocked;
  • a short log of the filter extension's own status (such as when it loaded the blocklist), used to show errors in the app; and
  • for Hush Everywhere, a cache of recent answers that iOS keeps itself.

None of this is sent to me. It is deleted when you delete the app, and you can remove your own hosts, your VPN configuration or Hush Everywhere from inside the app at any time.

Safari blocking

Hush's Safari extensions give Safari a list of rules, and Safari applies them itself. The extensions are never told which pages you visit, and nothing about your browsing reaches Hush or me.

VPN mode

VPN mode uses a VPN configuration on your device to check each DNS lookup (the step where an app turns a name like example.com into an address). Lookups for ads and trackers are answered on your device and never leave it. Hush doesn't log the names you look up.

  • On its own, only lookups pass through Hush. The ones it doesn't block are forwarded to Cloudflare's public DNS resolver (1.1.1.1). These lookups are not encrypted, so Cloudflare, and networks along the way, can see the names looked up and your IP address. Cloudflare handles them under its resolver privacy policy. All your other traffic goes out as usual, not through Hush.
  • With your own VPN provider, Hush connects to the provider you chose using the configuration you imported, and your traffic and lookups it doesn't block go to that provider, under its own privacy policy. If that configuration names no DNS server, Hush uses Cloudflare's, through your VPN.

No server of mine is involved in VPN mode.

Hush Everywhere

Hush Everywhere uses iOS's built-in URL filter. iOS itself does the checking; the Hush app and its extension never see the addresses your apps request.

  • Most requests never leave your device. Hush gives iOS a compact summary of the blocklist, and iOS rules out almost every address against it without asking anything.
  • The rest are asked about privately. When the summary can't rule an address out, iOS asks my filtering service at service.soumyamahunt.com using Private Information Retrieval: the question is encrypted so that the service can answer whether the address is blocked without learning which address was asked about.
  • The service doesn't know who is asking. In App Store and TestFlight builds, iOS sends these questions through Apple's Oblivious HTTP relay, so the service sees the relay rather than your IP address, and Apple's relay can't read the questions. Requests carry anonymous Privacy Pass tokens and an access token that is the same for every copy of Hush, so the service can't tell users apart or link questions to you.
  • When you switch Hush Everywhere on, the app sends one request to service.soumyamahunt.com to check that it can be reached. Like any web request it carries your IP address, but nothing about your browsing.

The filtering service runs on a server I operate on Oracle Cloud, with Cloudflare in front of it as a network proxy. Cloudflare and Oracle process this traffic under their own privacy policies. The service keeps no record of the addresses asked about, since it never learns them. Its operational logs record when requests arrive and which part of the service they used, so I can keep it running, and do not identify you.

Purchases

Hush itself and the Hush Everywhere subscription are sold and billed by Apple through the App Store. I never see your payment details or Apple Account. The app uses Apple's StoreKit on your device to check whether you're subscribed, using the transaction information Apple provides. You can manage or cancel it in your device's subscription settings.

Blocklist updates

The blocklists ship inside the app and are updated with new versions of it. Hush doesn't download blocklists from me or anyone else.

Analytics and tracking

Hush contains no analytics, crash reporting, advertising or other third-party services. It does not track you across other companies' apps or websites. If you choose to share diagnostics with app developers in iOS settings, Apple may share crash reports with me under the Apple Privacy Policy.

Children

Hush is a general-audience app and is not directed at children under 13. I don't knowingly collect personal data from anyone, including children.

Your rights

Depending on where you live (for example under the GDPR in Europe or privacy laws in some US states), you may have rights to access, delete, or object to the use of your personal data. Because I don't hold personal data about you, most of these are covered by deleting the app. For data handled by Apple, Cloudflare or your VPN provider, use their controls, or contact me and I'll point you to the right place.

Changes to this policy

If the app's data use changes, I'll update this page and the effective date above. Significant changes will also be noted in the App Store release notes.
